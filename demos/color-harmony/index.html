Palette Construction

One seed hue generates an entire product palette. The reason this works in OKLCH and not in HSL is that OKLCH lightness is perceptually uniform — equal steps look equally different, and changing hue at fixed lightness does not change apparent brightness.

1 · Harmony

Every scheme is just arithmetic on the hue angle. Nothing mystical — but keeping lightness and chroma fixed while only the hue moves is what makes the results feel like one family.

2 · The ramp, and why chroma must taper

Twelve lightness steps. Full chroma at 95% or 10% lightness does not exist in any display gamut — the browser clips it, and the top and bottom of your ramp turn to mud. Tapering chroma toward both ends keeps every step inside the gamut.

Dots mark swatches outside sRGB. They will still render on a P3 display; on an sRGB one the browser clips them, which is why two adjacent steps can look identical in the untapered row.

3 · Neutrals are not grey

Pure grey next to a saturated brand colour looks dead. Mixing a trace of the seed hue into the neutral ramp — a chroma of 0.004 to 0.02 — makes greys belong to the palette. Push the tint slider from 0.

4 · Roles, not colours

Components must never reference a ramp step directly. They reference a role — surface, raised, border, text, muted, accent, danger — and the role points at a step. That indirection is what lets you re-theme by changing one number, and it is the entire reason design tokens exist.

5 · Prove it is readable

Every text-on-background pair in the mock above, measured live. APCA Lc is the primary number: it models perceived contrast far better than the WCAG 2.1 ratio, which systematically overrates light-on-dark. WCAG is shown because it is still what most audits require.

Rules of thumb for APCA: Lc 90 for small body text, Lc 75 for larger body, Lc 60 for headlines, Lc 45 for large or non-essential text, Lc 30 is the floor for anything that must be perceived at all — including borders and disabled states.
